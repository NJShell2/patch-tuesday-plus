Patch now: the top 3

High

1. Frontline Education breach, 3,709 employees' SSNs exposed

Frontline Education notified customers of a breach hitting 3,709 past and present employees of one school corporation: names, Social Security numbers, addresses, and email addresses. Frontline says it has no evidence of misuse so far and is offering two years of TransUnion credit monitoring and identity-theft protection, with direct notification by email and U.S. mail. Individual exposure status was not confirmed for every affected person, so the true scope is still settling.

Who is affected: any organization running Frontline Education for HR and personnel management. If your shop uses their products, assume your current and former employees' SSNs are in the exposed set until your HR team confirms otherwise.

Action: Get HR and your staff into the TransUnion monitoring enrollment now, not later. Remind your helpdesk that exposed SSNs mean an identity-fraud wave, not a patch, so the defense is credit freezes and fraud alerts, not a software update.
High

2. AI-powered phishing surge during Cybersecurity Awareness Month

The old phishing tells are gone. AI-generated lures no longer have the typos and awkward phrasing that user training taught people to spot; instead they scrape LinkedIn titles and department names to look personal, and deepfake voice and video are now showing up in wire-transfer and gift-card scams. The standard advice, verify urgent requests through a second channel, matters more than ever because the message itself can no longer be trusted on appearance.

Who is affected: every organization with email and a finance department, which is all of them. Anyone who moves money on a phone call or a Teams message is a target.

Action: Send one awareness-month note to staff now: no money moves or credential sharing on a single channel, ever. Review your MFA registrations while you are at it, since stolen sessions are how these lures get cashed out.
Plan

3. Cisco Secure Client VPN sign-in moves to browser-based auth on October 13

Cisco Secure Client VPN sign-in shifts to a browser window or tab using SSO and Microsoft MFA on October 13. One important nuance: the My Sign-Ins default method no longer governs VPN authentication; Microsoft Entra ID picks the method from whatever each user has registered. Users with stale or missing MFA methods will get stuck at the new flow with no obvious fix.

Who is affected: shops running Cisco Secure Client with Entra SSO and Microsoft MFA. The wider pattern is worth noting for everyone else too: SSO vendors keep moving VPN auth into the browser, so your users will keep seeing this change somewhere.

Action: Audit registered MFA methods for your heavy VPN users this week, before the 13th. Send a heads-up describing the legitimate browser flow so nobody reports it as phishing, and staff the helpdesk for a ticket bump that week.
